# What Freedom Is For

*AI may give us more free time than any civilization has known. The harder problem will be learning how to live in it.*

Picture an ordinary citizen of the abundant future.

His AI has answered the email, reviewed the contract, booked the dentist, compared the mortgages, ordered the groceries, fixed the leaking faucet by dispatching a robot, and produced three tasteful birthday messages for relatives whose birthdays he would otherwise have discovered three days too late.

The machines have finished the work. The calendar lies open before him—gleaming! liberated! an entire evening returned to human sovereignty!

And there he is at 11:47 p.m., face illuminated by a six-inch rectangle, watching a stranger explain why another stranger’s refrigerator organization system is a sign of narcissistic personality disorder.

Free at last! Free to refresh.

Ten thousand years of toolmaking, agriculture, industry, electrification, computation, and artificial intelligence have reached their climax: humanity can now avoid opening the novel on the nightstand with unprecedented efficiency.

This is the strange problem waiting inside the promise of AI. The technology may eventually give us vast quantities of free time. But free time is not a finished human good, like clean water or penicillin. It is an empty container. It can hold friendship, craftsmanship, beauty, love, play, learning, adventure, contemplation, and rest.

It can also hold four hours of content that leaves behind no memory except the vague conviction that somebody, somewhere, is using an air fryer incorrectly.

**AI can make leisure abundant. It cannot make leisure good.**

## The problem Keynes saw coming

John Maynard Keynes saw the outline of this problem in 1930, while much of the world was busy collapsing into the Great Depression. In *Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren*, he predicted that technological progress might eventually reduce necessary labour to roughly three hours a day, or fifteen hours a week. By around 2030, he thought, the old struggle for subsistence could be largely solved in wealthy societies.

The prediction is usually presented as one of history’s more charmingly failed forecasts. We got the wealth. We did not get the fifteen-hour workweek.

But Keynes’s deeper observation was not about scheduling. It was about psychology. Once necessity retreated, he argued, humanity would confront its “permanent problem”: how to use freedom to live wisely and well. ([John Maynard Keynes][1])

The problem was even older than Keynes. Aristotle argued that education should prepare people not only to work well but to **use leisure well**. Work, in his account, was not the ultimate purpose of life. It was what made the rest of life possible. Leisure was not merely the recovery bay where workers were repaired before Monday morning. It was one of the places in which human flourishing actually occurred. ([Aristotle][2])

For most of history, this was a problem mainly for aristocrats, monks, scholars, and the occasionally fortunate goat herder. Everybody else had crops, children, animals, factories, ledgers, laundry, and the recurring nuisance of not starving.

AI changes the scale of the question.

Economic models of a transition to artificial general intelligence examine what happens when machines become capable of performing not merely particular jobs but essentially all economically useful tasks. In such models, production can cease to be constrained by the supply of human labour. Once intelligence and, eventually, physical action can be reproduced through software and machinery, immense output no longer requires immense human effort. ([Anton Korinek and Donghyun Suh][3])

No model can tell us precisely when this world arrives. Nor does one need to believe that next Tuesday’s chatbot will become the god-emperor of accounting. The long-run logic is enough: if AI and robotics can eventually perform most of the cognitive and physical work required to sustain material civilization, abundant discretionary time becomes less a utopian decoration than the natural residue of production no longer needing much from us.

So let us grant the premise. The machines become extraordinarily capable. Material abundance rises. Necessary human labour falls. Leisure becomes plentiful.

Then what?

That is the starting point of this article, not the conclusion.

## The junk drawer called “free time”

Industrial society gave us a magnificently crude theory of time.

There is **work**: employment, chores, obligations, maintenance, the school run, the dentist, the spreadsheet, the heroic Saturday afternoon spent trying to persuade a smoke detector that it has already received a new battery.

And then there is **free time**: everything else.

Into this conceptual junk drawer we toss:

* teaching a child to ride a bicycle;
* learning the cello;
* tending a garden;
* watching *The Godfather*;
* watching seventeen videos about celebrities we do not like;
* having dinner with old friends;
* getting drunk with old friends;
* getting drunk alone;
* volunteering at a food bank;
* building a table;
* playing a game;
* compulsively playing the same game at 2:40 a.m.;
* sleeping in the sun;
* reading philosophy;
* pretending to read philosophy while repeatedly checking email.

None of these activities appears on a payroll, so—into the drawer they go!

But “free time” tells us only where an activity sits on the calendar. It says almost nothing about the quality of the experience.

Leisure researchers have struggled for decades to define leisure by listing activities, and the attempt keeps collapsing for an obvious reason: almost any activity can be experienced as work, play, duty, pleasure, avoidance, or compulsion. Gardening can be a Saturday morning joy or an order barked by a homeowners’ association. Programming can be employment or creative play. Travel can be a dream vacation or a fourteen-hour business ordeal featuring fluorescent lighting and a man eating tuna beside Gate 17.

The more useful psychological distinction is subjective. Leisure involves some combination of perceived freedom, choice, and intrinsic motivation. It is something we experience as ours to enter—or not enter—rather than something imposed on us. ([Seppo Iso-Ahola and Roy Baumeister][4])

> **Free time is a vacancy in the calendar. Leisure is a way of inhabiting it.**

Even having a great deal of discretionary time does not automatically produce well-being. Psychologists sometimes use the term **time affluence** for the felt sense that one has enough time and meaningful control over it. Across four studies, perceived time affluence was positively associated with subjective well-being even after researchers accounted for material affluence. ([Tim Kasser and Kennon Sheldon][5])

But more is not endlessly better. Research drawing on two large datasets comprising more than 35,000 Americans, followed by experiments, found that very little discretionary time was associated with lower well-being—but extremely large amounts were not automatically blissful either. The downside of abundant time was reduced when that time was spent socially or in activities experienced as worthwhile. The precise hourly boundaries are not commandments carved onto a stone tablet; the important finding is that **quantity does not settle the question of quality**. ([Marissa Sharif, Cassie Mogilner and Hal Hershfield][6])

An empty afternoon can feel like a gift. It can also feel like a low-grade emergency.

Free time is the raw material of freedom, not its finished form.

## Life-enlarging leisure

Psychology already has a respectable term for the kind of life we are circling: **eudaimonic well-being**.

“Eudaimonic” sounds like either an Aristotelian concept or a boutique brand of probiotic skin cream. It refers to a life characterized not merely by pleasure but by meaning, growth, fuller functioning, and the realization of human capacities.

Pleasure matters. Happiness matters. But a good life is not simply the largest possible number of agreeable sensations stacked from birth to death like casino chips.

For ordinary prose, however, *eudaimonic leisure* is too scholarly and *valuable free time* sounds as if PricewaterhouseCoopers has begun auditing Sunday afternoons. A better term is **life-enlarging leisure**:

> **Life-enlarging leisure is freely chosen time that leaves us more restored, connected, capable, generative, expanded—or simply more alive.**

The word **leaves** matters.

The value of leisure is not determined by how impressive the activity looks on Instagram. It is partly revealed by its residue: what remains in the person, the relationship, or the surrounding world after the experience ends.

A widely used framework in leisure psychology proposes that leisure supports well-being through detachment and recovery, autonomy, mastery, meaning, and affiliation. The acronym is DRAMMA, which sounds suspiciously like either a Scandinavian pop group or what happens when somebody brings a board game with a forty-page rulebook to the cottage. But the underlying categories are useful. ([David Newman, Louis Tay and Ed Diener][7])

Translated into less acronymic language, leisure enlarges life in five major ways.

![People cooking, making pottery, cycling, playing music, and resting together in a courtyard](./images/02__what_freedom_for.webp)

### It restores us

Some leisure returns the nervous system to its owner.

A walk. A bath. Music. Exercise. Quiet television. A nap. Sitting beside water. Looking at trees without immediately trying to identify, rank, monetize, or photograph them.

Restoration is not a consolation prize awarded to people too exhausted for woodworking. It is a basic function of leisure. Detachment from demands and genuine relaxation help replenish the psychological resources consumed by effort and attention.

A society obsessed with productivity tends to treat rest as maintenance: sleep so you can perform; vacation so you can return “recharged”; meditate so quarterly revenue can enjoy a calmer nervous system.

But rest need not submit a business case. Being restored is itself part of living well.

### It connects us

A large share of life’s meaning is not privately manufactured inside the skull. It is produced between people.

Leisure is where relationships escape logistics. Families stop coordinating and begin living together. Friends move beyond status updates. Couples become something other than co-managers of a household supply chain.

Shared meals, walks, trips, games, celebrations, rituals, clubs, projects, and long conversations do not merely occupy time. They create a common history. They produce jokes that make no sense to outsiders, stories retold for decades, and the quiet accumulation of trust.

A magical evening may leave behind no object, no certification, and no improved resting heart rate. It may leave behind a friendship that feels different from that day onward.

That counts.

### It develops us

Some leisure expands what we can do.

We learn to paint, cook, dance, write, repair, garden, lift, sing, speak another language, study history, play Go, restore furniture, identify birds, or make sourdough with the grave moral seriousness previously reserved for constitutional law.

These activities often have an inconvenient feature: they require effort.

The guitar does not play itself. The difficult novel does not crawl into the mind. The first pottery bowl resembles an object recovered from a minor archaeological disaster.

Yet effort appears to work differently in leisure than it does in obligatory work. Across five studies, researchers found that more effortful leisure tended to feel more meaningful while retaining enjoyment; in work and chores, effort was more likely to reduce enjoyment. The relationship was not limitless—misery does not become enlightenment merely because it is difficult—but the research supports a familiar experience: many of the activities hardest to begin become deeply satisfying once underway. ([Aidan Campbell, Gregory Depow and Michael Inzlicht][8])

### It generates something

Human beings do not merely consume worlds. We like to add something to them.

A poem. A garden. A meal. A piece of software. A repaired chair. A neighbourhood gathering. A family tradition. A volunteer organization. A ridiculous treehouse that violates several principles of structural engineering but becomes the centre of a child’s summer.

Generative leisure need not produce a marketable artifact. It can generate culture, memory, capability, belonging, or care.

In a 13-day diary study of 658 young adults, people reported greater activated positive emotion and flourishing on days following more creative activity than was usual for them. The design does not prove that creativity is a universal happiness machine, but it supports the idea that ordinary acts of making can alter the texture of everyday life. ([Tamlin Conner, Colin DeYoung and Paul Silvia][9])

Creation says: *I was here, and while I was here, something came into existence.*

### It expands us

Some experiences enlarge the world itself.

A journey. A strange friendship. A difficult film. A museum. A novel whose characters continue arguing inside the mind for years. A piece of music that gives form to a feeling we had never been able to name.

Psychologists Shigehiro Oishi and Erin Westgate call this **psychological richness**: a dimension of the good life distinct from both happiness and meaning, characterized by variety, complexity, novelty, interest, and changes in perspective. ([Shigehiro Oishi and Erin Westgate][10])

The psychologically rich life is not necessarily peaceful. Some experiences are valuable because they disturb the furniture.

They show us an unfamiliar way to live, reveal that an old certainty was provincial, or make the world larger than the room in which we had arranged it.

## There is no approved list

At this point, the article is in danger.

A committee forms. Clipboards appear. Somebody creates a colour-coded chart:

**GREEN LEISURE:** running, reading, gardening, volunteering.
**YELLOW LEISURE:** television, gaming, dessert.
**RED LEISURE:** alcohol, social media, reality television, anything enjoyed by people wearing sweatpants.

This would be tidy, actionable—and mostly wrong.

There is no reliable list of activities that enlarge life in every context. The visible activity does not tell us the person’s motivation, attention, dosage, social setting, or relationship to it.

Running can bring vitality, mastery, and friendship. It can also become a compulsive system of self-punishment that colonizes meals, family plans, injuries, and self-worth.

Building a company on weekends can be joyous creation. It can also be workaholism wearing a Patagonia vest.

Reading can deepen a life. It can also be avoidance performed with superior typography.

Psychologist Robert Vallerand’s distinction between **harmonious** and **obsessive** passion is useful here. Harmonious passion is voluntarily integrated into a wider life. Obsessive passion contains rigidity, pressure, and conflict with other important values. The activity may be identical; the relationship to it is not. ([Robert Vallerand and colleagues][11])

Television illustrates the same ambiguity. Research comparing television with other leisure activities found that it could provide comparable relaxation and detachment, although it generally supplied less mastery, meaning, affiliation, and satisfaction. In other words: television can genuinely restore us, but it is less likely than some other pursuits to satisfy the full range of psychological needs. ([Lauren Kuykendall and colleagues][12])

That does not make *The Sopranos* morally inferior to pickleball. It means the role television plays in a life depends on what else is there—and what television is replacing.

Even drinking, the classic exhibit in every sermon on wasted leisure, refuses to stay inside its assigned box.

In a large experiment, researchers placed 720 previously unacquainted social drinkers into three-person groups and gave them alcoholic, placebo, or nonalcoholic control drinks. Moderate alcohol increased positive social behaviour and self-reported bonding during the group’s first interaction. That is not evidence that alcohol is healthy, necessary for intimacy, or free of enormous risks. It is evidence for the narrower and more interesting point that an apparently indulgent activity can sometimes participate in a genuinely connective experience. ([Michael Sayette and colleagues][13])

A night of drinking may be empty repetition, avoidance, dependency, or damage.

It may also be the night two friends finally speak honestly, a family tells the old stories again, strangers become friends, everybody laughs until their faces hurt, and someone walks home under the streetlights feeling—for once—completely inside his own life.

The drink does not settle the category. The whole experience does.

> **A beer can enlarge a life. A marathon can shrink one.**

The same activity can be nourishment on Saturday and anaesthetic on Tuesday.

The correct unit of analysis is not the activity. It is the relationship a person has formed with it.

## In defence of necessary noise

Now come the productivity police.

They have heard about life-enlarging leisure and are delighted. At last! A framework! Sunday afternoon can be optimized. A Notion template can be constructed. Every hobby can have key performance indicators. The children’s playdate can be followed by a retrospective.

What were our learnings from the picnic?

Could the nap have been more generative?

Did the glass of wine advance Q3 flourishing objectives?

This is exactly the wrong conclusion.

Life-enlarging leisure is not an attempt to turn free time into unpaid work. It does not mean that every evening must improve a skill, generate an artifact, strengthen a relationship, reveal a philosophical truth, and end with a handwritten gratitude practice beneath artisanal lighting.

Some leisure does not need to improve you.

It only needs to be enjoyed.

Research bears this out. Across four studies involving 1,310 participants, people who believed leisure was wasteful enjoyed it less. Experimentally encouraging participants to see leisure as unproductive also reduced their enjoyment, while the broader belief was associated with lower happiness and greater depression, anxiety, and stress. ([Gabriela Tonietto and colleagues][14])

The attempt to justify every pleasure by its future usefulness destroys something essential about pleasure. The idle walk must secretly support creativity. The holiday must prevent burnout. The dinner with friends must improve longevity. The nap must sharpen executive function.

Soon we are no longer living. We are servicing the equipment.

A good life needs what we might call **recreational slack**: pleasure, amusement, softness, idleness, and spontaneity without an improvement agenda.

Watch the silly show. Eat the dessert. Browse the bookstore without buying the definitive volume that will complete your intellectual architecture. Lie in the sun. Play a game badly. Spend an hour talking about nothing important with someone important.

This is the necessary noise of a human life.

In music, silence between every note would not create purity. It would create a very slow and irritating concert. Life also needs connective tissue, ornament, digression, laughter, and waste in the old sense of extravagance—more than is strictly required.

Not every hour needs a performance review.

## The real opposite of good leisure

The opposite of life-enlarging leisure is not pleasure.

It is not laziness.

It is not television, alcohol, gaming, passive entertainment, or an afternoon during which the most ambitious act is turning over to keep the sun out of one eye.

The opposite is **capture**.

> **Captured leisure is time nominally under our control but practically selected by exhaustion, habit, compulsion, social pressure, autoplay, or an algorithm—and often continued after enjoyment has ended.**

The distinction is easy to feel.

Absorption says: *I gave myself fully to something I value.*

Capture says: *I continued giving attention after the experience stopped serving anything I value.*

The first can happen while reading a novel, playing a game, watching a film, coding, dancing, or talking with a friend. The second can happen during exactly the same activities.

Capture is not defined by a screen. It is defined by weakened agency.

But screens have become rather good at producing it.

In one field study of social-media design, researchers examined what they called **normative dissociation**: a nonclinical state of absorption marked by reduced awareness of oneself and one’s actions—the familiar experience of emerging from a feed with little recollection of what one actually consumed. Interface changes such as custom lists, reading-history labels, time-limit prompts, and usage statistics reduced these experiences, suggesting that disappearing into a feed is not simply a pristine expression of consumer preference. Design helps produce the behaviour. ([Amanda Baughan and colleagues][15])

And now AI enters—not only as the great liberator of labour, but as the greatest potential manufacturer of distraction ever devised.

Today’s entertainment industry is still constrained by human production. Somebody has to write the episode, record the song, edit the video, build the game, invent the personalities, and manufacture the outrage.

Generative AI loosens that constraint. It can produce media continuously, cheaply, interactively, and eventually in response to each person’s preferences, mood, loneliness, arousal, boredom, history, and moment-to-moment behaviour.

Experiments have already shown that generative AI can automate psychologically personalized persuasive messages at scale. The reasonable inference is not that every AI-generated story or companion will be sinister. It is that the same capacity can make entertainment and persuasion extraordinarily adaptive to the individual receiving them. ([Sandra Matz and colleagues][16])

The AI age may therefore give us two things at once:

1. unprecedented abundance of time;
2. unprecedented machinery for consuming it.

The old problem was finding something to do.

The new problem may be preserving enough sovereignty over attention to do anything that was not selected for us.

## How to build a richer leisure life

The answer is not to become constantly vigilant, squinting suspiciously at every sitcom and glass of Scotch.

Nor is it to construct a moral hierarchy in which one earns ninety minutes of “bad leisure” by first completing sixty minutes of improving leisure, like a Victorian child being permitted jam after Latin.

The goal is not control over every minute. It is authorship over the shape of one’s life.

A useful way to begin is to ask what kind of experience is actually needed.

### Start with the need, not the activity

When people ask, “What should I do tonight?” they usually search an inventory of activities.

Netflix? Gym? Book? Game? Friends? The unfinished project glowering from the garage?

A better first question is:

> **What do I need from leisure tonight?**

Do I need to be restored?

Do I need connection?

Do I want challenge and mastery?

Do I want to make something?

Do I need novelty or a change in perspective?

Or do I simply need recreational slack—a gentle evening that asks nothing more of me?

This prevents the absurdity of choosing “high-value” leisure that is badly mismatched to the person. A thoroughly depleted parent does not necessarily need to develop watercolour technique. A lonely retiree may need a recurring group more than another solitary hobby. An executive who spends all week manipulating abstractions may need movement, touch, dirt, food, music, or laughter—not a second shift of intellectual development.

### Build a portfolio, not a regime

Over a month or a season, a rich leisure life will usually include more than one kind of good.

Some restoration. Some relationship. Some skill. Some creation. Some novelty. Some indulgence. Some open space in which nothing has already been decided.

Call it a **leisure portfolio**, provided nobody immediately builds an app that assigns each Sunday a diversification score.

The proportions will change by life stage. New parents may need vastly more restoration. Adolescents need exploration and social belonging. A newly retired person may need structure, identity, and serious pursuits. Someone recovering from burnout may need to rediscover desire before deciding what to do with it.

The metaphor is meant to expose what is missing, not to create quotas.

### Lower the cost of beginning

Life-enlarging leisure frequently suffers from a start-up problem.

The guitar must be taken out. The friend must be called. The materials must be assembled. The class requires a drive. The difficult book demands ten minutes before its world opens. The beginner must endure the intolerable indignity of being visibly mediocre.

Captured leisure has none of these defects.

It is right there! Warm, frictionless, infinitely replenished. One tap and—whoosh!—the evening slides down the polished chute.

This is why the answer is rarely a heroic speech about willpower. The environment should make meaningful activities easier to begin.

Leave the instrument visible. Keep the book where you sit. Arrange the workspace before you need it. Join a recurring class. Establish a standing dinner. Make plans with another person. Define a tiny first step.

Research on **implementation intentions**—simple if-then plans linking an action to a concrete cue—has found a substantial effect on translating intentions into behaviour. A meta-analysis covering 94 independent tests found a medium-to-large average improvement in goal attainment. “I should paint more” is an aspiration. “After breakfast on Saturday, I will paint for twenty minutes at the kitchen table” is a beginning. ([Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran][17])

### Craft leisure deliberately—but not neurotically

Leisure researchers use the term **leisure crafting** for proactively shaping free time around learning, personal development, meaningful goals, and human connection.

A randomized intervention found increases in meaning, self-efficacy, work engagement, and creativity among participants encouraged to craft their leisure more deliberately. The evidence is promising rather than final; it does not yet justify a $499 Leisure Optimization Masterclass featuring downloadable worksheets and a photograph of a man looking purposeful beside a mountain. ([Paraskevas Petrou and colleagues][18])

The useful idea is modest: fulfilling leisure does not always arrive spontaneously after work. Sometimes it must be made possible in advance.

We schedule obligations because they matter. We often leave relationships, interests, beauty, and play to whatever exhausted scraps remain.

Then we are astonished when the scraps are eaten by the easiest available thing.

### Audit patterns, not evenings

No isolated night needs to justify itself.

A lost evening may be exactly what a person needed. Three hours of ridiculous television can be glorious. A party can run too late. A game can consume a weekend. A holiday can contain an entire day whose only achievement is deciding where to eat.

The useful unit of analysis is the pattern.

Four questions can expose it:

1. **Origin:** Did I choose this, or did a default effectively choose it for me?
2. **Experience:** Was I present, amused, absorbed, connected, challenged, or restored?
3. **Residue:** What remained afterward—energy, closeness, skill, perspective, satisfaction, dullness, agitation?
4. **Displacement:** What does this habit repeatedly crowd out?

The fourth question is the brutal one.

The problem with scrolling is not that every minute is worthless. The problem is that it may repeatedly displace the friend we wanted to call, the book we wanted to read, the sleep we needed, the project we claim matters, or even the quieter entertainment we would have enjoyed more.

> **Do not audit every hour. Audit what repeatedly crowds out what.**

## Leisure literacy

Industrial civilization became extremely good at preparing people for work.

We teach children to read instructions, meet deadlines, follow schedules, acquire credentials, specialize, collaborate, compete, sit still, look busy, and use Microsoft Teams while maintaining the facial expression of a person whose soul remains technically under warranty.

We teach professional literacy.

But if abundant leisure becomes a central condition of human life, another form of education becomes necessary.

The term already exists: **leisure literacy**.

Researchers have used it to describe the knowledge, skills, and confidence needed to engage in personally meaningful leisure. Leisure education can include learning to identify one’s needs and interests, recognize available opportunities, overcome constraints, plan activities, and participate with greater competence and autonomy. ([Susan Hutchinson and Brenda Robertson][19])

The evidence base is real but not yet sweeping. An integrative review found 64 studies of leisure education across varied populations and outcomes, but the studies were heterogeneous, often small, and concentrated in North America. Leisure literacy is a serious research field, not a settled universal curriculum waiting to be photocopied into every school. ([Shintaro Kono, Chungsup Lee and John Dattilo][20])

For an age of abundance, the concept should be widened:

> **Leisure literacy is the ability to know what we need from free time, choose experiences accordingly, overcome the friction of beginning, participate with attention, and recognize whether our patterns are enlarging our lives or consuming them.**

It includes the ability to:

* rest without guilt;
* play without turning play into achievement;
* develop interests rather than waiting to be stimulated;
* tolerate being a beginner;
* sustain friendships beyond occasional messages;
* establish rituals and communities;
* acquire taste and discernment;
* pursue difficult pleasures;
* leave enough open space for spontaneity;
* recognize when absorption has become capture.

This is not a minor lifestyle skill.

In an abundant society, leisure may become the primary arena in which people form identity, relationships, competence, culture, and meaning. Work will no longer be able to carry so much of the load—perhaps mercifully. But when work retreats, the human needs it imperfectly served do not vanish with the commute.

People will still need structure.

They will still need to feel capable.

They will still need other people to expect their presence.

They will still need projects larger than immediate appetite.

They will still need some answer to the question: *What am I doing with myself?*

The old work-centred civilization answered that question before most people had time to ask it. Here is your station. Your shift. Your profession. Your title. Your ladder. Your Friday. Your retirement.

The abundant future may remove that answer and hand back the question.

What looks from a distance like liberation may feel, at first, like vertigo.

## The first-person work of living

For most of human history, necessity decided what people would do with their days.

Food had to be grown. Water carried. Fires maintained. Children protected. Machines operated. Records kept. Wages earned. The next task stood waiting before the last one was finished.

Advanced AI may gradually weaken necessity’s command. It may open more of the day to genuine choice than any previous civilization has known.

But an open day is not yet a meaningful day.

Free time can become friendship, mastery, creation, beauty, celebration, adventure, contemplation, play, or genuine rest. It can also dissolve into an endless stream of experiences selected not because we wanted them, but because they were easiest to continue.

The challenge will not be finding things for human beings to do.

There are more people to love, abilities to develop, places to understand, stories to hear, questions to investigate, traditions to preserve, games to play, meals to share, promises to keep, and forms of beauty to encounter than any single life could contain.

The challenge will be learning to choose among them.

The machines may eventually perform nearly everything human beings once **had** to do. They cannot perform the irreducibly first-person work of living for us.

They cannot love our friends.

They cannot inhabit our bodies.

They cannot acquire our tastes.

They cannot encounter beauty on our behalf.

They cannot decide what deserves our devotion.

They cannot become the person each of us might become.

**AI can give us time. It cannot tell us what that time is for.**
